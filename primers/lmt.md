### Business and segments

Lockheed Martin is an aerospace and defense company headquartered in Bethesda, Maryland[^1]. The 10-K for the fiscal year ended December 31, 2025 states that it operates in four business segments: Aeronautics, Missiles and Fire Control (MFC), Rotary and Mission Systems (RMS) and Space[^1]. Segments are organized by the nature of the products and services offered[^1].

Aeronautics researches, designs, develops, manufactures, integrates, sustains and upgrades advanced military aircraft, including combat and air mobility aircraft and unmanned air vehicles[^1]. It also holds classified US Government contracts[^1]. Its major programs are the F-35, C-130, F-16 and F-22[^1]. The F-35 is the largest program, generating 27% of total consolidated sales and 67% of Aeronautics sales in 2025[^1]. The 10-K also states that the company may make strategic changes, acquisitions or divestitures to its organizational structure and portfolio[^1].

The 10-K reports 123,000 employees for FY2025[^1].

## 3. Products, Services and Technology

*Caveat: product and program descriptions are the company's own 10-K language and the latest earnings release; status reflects the words used in the filing, not an independent programme assessment, and trade-press pages are context only.*

### Portfolio by segment and status

Lockheed Martin reports four segments: Aeronautics, Missiles and Fire Control (MFC), Rotary and Mission Systems (RMS) and Space[^1]. The 10-K lists Aeronautics' major programs as the F-35 Lightning II, C-130 Hercules, F-16 Fighting Falcon and F-22 Raptor, and it also lists the CH-53K[^1].

The F-35 produced 27% of consolidated 2025 sales and 67% of Aeronautics sales[^1]. The program runs concurrent development, production and sustainment contracts[^1]. The U.S. Government's stated objective is 2,456 aircraft, and in 2025 seven partner countries and 12 FMS customers indicated intent to buy 72 more[^1]. Lockheed delivered a record 191 F-35s in 2025, up from 110 in 2024; the previous high was 142 in 2021[^2][^21]. Derived: 2025 deliveries exceeded the company's stated 156-per-year capacity by 35 (191 − 156), a gap Breaking Defense attributes to jets released from TR-3 storage[^2]. TR-3 aircraft remain limited to training and have not been declared combat capable[^2]. Nearly 1,300 F-35s are operational, according to a company release[^2].

F-16 deliveries resumed, and C-130 output increased; two F-16s and seven C-130s were delivered in Q2 2026[^9]. The second SPY-7 radar ship set for Japan's missile defense destroyers was delivered on schedule[^9]. The sources reviewed give no production metric for the F-22 or CH-53K.

In MFC, the U.S. Army awarded a $3 billion GMLRS contract covering the current rocket and a successor with twice the range[^9]. HIMARS received an award of up to $1.1 billion[^9]. In Space, Conventional Prompt Strike, a sea-based hypersonic program, received cumulative modifications of $1.4 billion[^9]. A $2.3 billion radar contract was also signed[^9]. Q2 2026 backlog reached a record $230 billion[^8].

### Milestones and production-system changes

On January 6, 2026, a seven-year framework agreed to raise PAC-3 MSE output to over 2,000 a year, more than triple the existing rate[^22]. NAM puts the starting rate at approximately 600[^6]. Defense News reported a $4.7 billion PAC-3 deal on April 10, 2026[^23]. In July, the Army converted an earlier agreement into a seven-year production contract of approximately $58.6 billion[^6]. In late June, the Missile Defense Agency awarded a seven-year, $35 billion contract to quadruple THAAD interceptor production[^9][^8].

In October 2026, Lockheed committed $300 million to its Grand Prairie, Texas, MFC plant[^6]. As part of that expansion, Hadrian will install 24 automated machines, which are expected to start production in early 2027[^6]. The company reports that its Sanctum counter-drone system went from concept to live-fire testing in 45 days[^8]. In August 2026, Lockheed launched a modular-missile "Strigo Product Center" and pitched an interceptor it says will cost less than half as much as a PAC-3 MSE[^24].

On F-35 capacity, an executive said in November 2025 that 156 a year is a "smart" rate rather than a maximum, and that the center fuselage is no longer a bottleneck[^25]. Lot 19 covered 296 jets, 40 of them for the Air Force[^25].

### Next-generation competitions and competitors

The 10-K names Boeing, General Dynamics, L3Harris, Northrop Grumman and RTX as competitors[^1]. Lockheed bid for the Navy's F/A-XX but was eliminated by March 2025, leaving Northrop Grumman and Boeing[^26]. Boeing won the Air Force NGAD fighter, and Lockheed said on April 22, 2025 that it would not protest[^27]. Instead, Lockheed plans to port its NGAD technologies into the F-35 and F-22[^27]. Its CEO claimed this could deliver 80% of NGAD capability at 50% of unit cost[^27]. The Space Force selected Lockheed as one of several awardees for Golden Dome space-based interceptor prototypes, with a demonstration expected by 2028[^9]. An Air Force planning document pairs the F-35A with Boeing's F-15EX and says both must be produced at maximum rates to reach "acceptable" risk[^25].

### Deal pattern and size
Lockheed Martin operates four segments: Aeronautics, Missiles and Fire Control, Rotary and Mission Systems (RMS) and Space[^1]. Its recent acquisitions have been bolt-on capability purchases rather than transformational mergers. The completed $360 million Rapid Solutions deal targeted radar and payload technology for the Space segment[^1]. On July 6, 2026, the company signed a definitive agreement to acquire Ultra Maritime, an undersea and anti-submarine warfare (ASW) specialist, for $3.45 billion[^61]. Ultra Maritime makes sonar, sonobuoys, torpedo defense, radar and autonomous maritime sensing systems[^61]. The RMS president announced the deal, which points to an RMS fit, although the release does not formally assign a segment[^61]. Derived: Ultra Maritime is about 9.6x the Rapid Solutions price ($3.45 billion ÷ $0.36 billion). Tracxn counts 16 Lockheed Martin acquisitions across 12 sectors[^62].

### Filings and disclosure
Every Item 1.01 8-K reviewed for 2022–2026 covers revolving credit agreements, amendments or extensions, not acquisitions[^63][^64][^65][^66][^67][^68]. No individual venture investment or divestiture is itemized in the sources reviewed[^1].

### Pending and terminated deals
Ultra Maritime remains at the signed-agreement stage in the sources reviewed, and no completion date is disclosed[^61]. In July 1998, Lockheed Martin abandoned an $8.3 billion merger with Northrop Grumman after government concerns that the combined group would control 25% of the Department of Defense's procurement budget[^69]. Earlier, in April 1996, the company completed the $9.1 billion purchase of Loral Corporation's defense electronics and systems integration businesses[^69].

## 12. Investment Thesis and Debates

*Caveat: No sell-side research or expert-call transcripts were available; firm names appear only where a cited transcript or page names the firm, rating actions are as listed on the aggregator page on the date shown, analyst questions are quoted as asked, not as views, and management answers are the company's statements on the same call, quoted from the same transcript.*

### Where the debate stands

The bull case rests on backlog and munitions demand. Backlog reached a record $230B in Q2 2026, and management raised 2026 guidance to about 8% sales growth at the midpoint[^9]. Q2 2026 sales rose 11% year over year to $20.1B, with a 10.8% segment margin and EPS of $7.94[^9]. This followed a flat Q1 2026, when sales were $18B and segment profit fell on non-recurring events and adverse profit adjustments[^85]. Backlog grew $17.3B, or 17%, during 2025[^79].

On munitions, the company announced a $300 million investment to accelerate U.S. missile production[^6]. MarketBeat links that investment to raising PAC-3 MSE output from about 600 units a year to at least 2,000 over time[^93]. The dividend was raised 4.3% to $3.60 per quarter, extending a 24-year growth streak[^93].

The bear case centres on execution and leverage. The company took charges on the Aeronautics and MFC classified programs in Q4 2024[^79]. It took further steps in Q2 2025 to reduce risk on Aeronautics and Sikorsky programs[^79]. On NGAD, Taiclet said "we weren't selected." He framed the response as using NGAD technology to make the F-35 a "bridge" from fifth to sixth generation[^94]. The 10-K identifies the F-35 as a material portion of revenue and lists funding, schedule and cost risk on that program[^1]. Short interest was 1.19% of float as of September 30[^95].

### What analysts asked and what management answered

- **Classified program.** On the Q4 2025 call, TD Cowen asked for an update on the Aero classified program[^79]. Taiclet said risk "is gonna remain over the next few years," that no additional charges were recorded in Q4, and that top executives are personally involved[^79]. On the Q3 2025 call, Bernstein asked how investors could get comfortable that program issues were behind the company[^80]. Taiclet said the company wanted "to put every risk that we can quantify behind us"[^80].
- **Munitions multiyears.** Seaport Research and Bernstein asked about the timing and structure of the PAC-3 and THAAD framework deals[^79]. Taiclet said definitization requires approved appropriations, so timing "will be up to the congressional budget cycle"[^79]. He added that seven-year authorization for four or five missile systems is advancing[^79].
- **Supply chain.** RBC Capital Markets asked whether the supply chain can support a high-single to low-double-digit growth outlook[^80]. Taiclet said he was "much more confident today than I was a year or so ago," citing seeker-supplier commitments[^80].
- **Golden Dome.** JPMorgan asked whether the prior multi-year trending still held[^80]. CFO Evan Scott said the trending was unchanged but that new opportunity was emerging around munitions and Golden Dome[^80].
- **Capital return.** Melius Research and Vertical Research asked whether the policy of returning 100% of free cash flow, and the buyback and dividend, would change after the executive orders[^79]. Taiclet said the process stays "disciplined and dynamic," that long-term contracts may justify more investment, and that decisions will be disclosed as they occur[^79].

- **Munitions contracts.** Whether the PAC-3 and THAAD frameworks become definitized multiyear contracts remains open. Management tied this to appropriations[^79]. Definitized awards in company or Pentagon announcements would settle it.
